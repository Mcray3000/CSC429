\begin{abstract}
Privacy and utility remain important concerns in federated intrusion detection
for Internet-of-Healthcare-Things (IoHT) networks, especially when clients are
heterogeneous and hold non-IID local data. Although federated learning (FL)
reduces raw data exposure, client model updates can still leak sensitive
information. Differential privacy (DP) is a widely used defense, but its utility
impact can vary substantially across datasets and client conditions. In this
work, we study adaptive privacy mechanisms for federated IoHT intrusion
detection under heterogeneous clients. We simulate non-IID client populations
using Dirichlet-based partitioning on the ECU-IoHT and WUSTL-EHMS 2020 datasets,
and compare no-DP training, fixed-noise DP, smoothed adaptive noise scaling, and
adaptive clipping. To quantify privacy leakage, we also implement a white-box
membership inference attack and evaluate attack performance on held-out splits.
Our results show that strict no-identifier preprocessing reduces membership
inference performance close to chance, while identifier-inclusive WUSTL serves
as a strong leakage-positive control. On the utility side, ECU remains near
ceiling across privacy mechanisms, limiting the room for adaptive methods to
show benefit, whereas WUSTL is substantially more sensitive to privacy
perturbations. Overall, we do not observe consistent or substantial utility
gains from adaptive privacy mechanisms across datasets; instead, the results
suggest that their effectiveness is highly dataset-dependent and may require
more challenging benchmarks or better-calibrated settings to produce meaningful
improvements.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Internet-of-Healthcare-Things (IoHT) devices are increasingly used to monitor
patients, transmit medical data, and support connected healthcare services. The
data generated by these systems can improve machine learning-based intrusion
detection, but centralizing sensitive medical and network data raises
significant privacy concerns. Federated learning (FL) reduces this risk by
allowing clients to train locally and share model updates rather than raw data.
However, model updates can still leak information about local training samples.

Differential privacy (DP) is commonly used to reduce such leakage by perturbing
client updates before aggregation. Many DP-based FL methods apply a fixed noise
level to all clients, but this is limiting for heterogeneous IoHT clients with
different data volumes, class balances, and traffic distributions. In intrusion
detection, where malicious samples may be rare and unevenly distributed, a
single fixed privacy setting can produce an unfavorable privacy--utility
tradeoff.

Adaptive privacy mechanisms address this limitation by adjusting perturbation
across clients or training rounds. Prior work has shown the promise of adaptive
local DP in FL, but often on general image-classification benchmarks rather than
heterogeneous IoHT intrusion-detection datasets. Thus, the practical benefit of
adaptive privacy for federated IoHT intrusion detection remains
under-characterized.

In this work, we evaluate fixed and adaptive client-side privacy mechanisms for
federated IoHT intrusion detection. We use Dirichlet-based partitioning to
simulate non-IID clients on the ECU-IoHT and WUSTL-EHMS 2020 datasets, and
compare no-DP training, fixed-noise DP, smoothed adaptive noise scaling, and
adaptive clipping. We evaluate detection utility using precision, recall,
F1-score, and ROC-AUC, and assess privacy leakage using a white-box membership
inference attack.

Our main contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item We evaluate fixed and adaptive client-side privacy mechanisms under
heterogeneous, non-IID federated IoHT clients.
\item We compare privacy--utility behavior on ECU-IoHT and WUSTL-EHMS 2020 using
intrusion-detection metrics and white-box membership inference.
\item We find that adaptive privacy mechanisms provide similar utility to
fixed-noise DP in our current settings, while membership inference results are
strongly influenced by preprocessing choices such as identifier removal.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section II reviews related
work. Section III presents the methodology. Section IV describes the experimental
setup. Section V presents the evaluation. Section VI concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}

Federated learning has been widely studied as a privacy-preserving approach for
distributed machine learning, especially in settings where raw data cannot be
centralized. In healthcare and IoHT environments, FL is attractive because
medical and network traffic data may contain sensitive information. However,
federated learning alone does not guarantee privacy, since model updates can
still reveal information about local training records.

Differential privacy is often used to mitigate this risk by clipping model
updates and adding calibrated noise before aggregation. Fixed-noise DP methods
are simple and widely used, but they may not be optimal when clients differ in
data size, label distribution, and update behavior. Adaptive privacy mechanisms
attempt to improve this tradeoff by changing clipping thresholds or noise levels
over clients or training rounds.

Membership inference attacks provide a practical way to evaluate privacy
leakage. In these attacks, an adversary attempts to determine whether a specific
record was included in the model's training data. White-box attacks are
especially relevant in federated settings because the adversary may have access
to model parameters, gradients, losses, or other training-related signals.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{System Model}

We consider a federated IoHT intrusion-detection setting with \(K\)
heterogeneous clients. Each client represents a local healthcare environment,
such as a glucose-monitoring system, blood-pressure monitoring system, or
patient-monitoring system. Client \(k\) holds a private local dataset \(D_k\),
which may differ from other clients in size, class balance, and traffic
distribution. Raw IoHT traffic and training records remain local to each client
throughout training.

A central federated server coordinates training but does not receive raw data.
In each communication round, selected clients train a local IDS model on their
private data, compute a model update, apply the assigned privacy mechanism, and
send only the resulting update to the server. The server aggregates client
updates to produce a global IDS model, which is then redistributed to the
clients.

\subsection{Federated Client Construction}

The ECU-IoHT and WUSTL-EHMS 2020 datasets are originally centralized. To
simulate heterogeneous federated clients, we partition the training data using a
Dirichlet distribution over class labels. For each class, samples are
distributed across \(K\) clients according to proportions drawn from
\[
p \sim \mathrm{Dirichlet}(\alpha),
\]
where \(\alpha\) controls the degree of non-IID heterogeneity. Smaller values of
\(\alpha\) produce more imbalanced client class distributions. In our
experiments, this process creates clients with different data volumes and
different proportions of normal and malicious samples, reflecting realistic IoHT
deployment conditions.

\subsection{Federated IDS Training}

Let \(\theta^t\) denote the global model parameters at round \(t\). At the start
of each round, the server broadcasts \(\theta^t\) to participating clients. Each
client trains locally on its private dataset \(D_k\) and obtains updated local
parameters \(\theta_k^t\). The client update is defined as
\[
\Delta_k^t = \theta_k^t - \theta^t.
\]
The server aggregates client updates using FedAvg:
\[
\theta^{t+1} = \theta^t + \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k \widetilde{\Delta}_k^t,
\]
where \(w_k\) is the aggregation weight for client \(k\), and
\(\widetilde{\Delta}_k^t\) is the privacy-protected update sent by the client.

\subsection{Client-Side Privacy Mechanisms}

We compare four client-side privacy settings: no-DP training, fixed-noise DP,
smoothed adaptive noise scaling, and adaptive clipping.

For DP-based methods, each client update is first clipped to limit its
sensitivity:
\[
\bar{\Delta}_k^t =
\Delta_k^t \cdot
\min\left(1, \frac{C_t}{\left\lVert \Delta_k^t \right\rVert_2}\right),
\]
where \(C_t\) is the clipping threshold. Gaussian noise is then added to the
clipped update:
\[
\widetilde{\Delta}_k^t =
\bar{\Delta}_k^t + \mathcal{N}\left(0, \sigma_{k,t}^{2} C_t^{2} I\right).
\]
In fixed-noise DP, the same noise multiplier is used for all clients and rounds:
\[
\sigma_{k,t} = \sigma.
\]
In smoothed adaptive noise scaling, the noise multiplier changes according to
the smoothed client update norm. Let
\[
s_{k,t} = \lambda s_{k,t-1} + (1-\lambda)\left\lVert \Delta_k^t \right\rVert_2
\]
be the exponentially smoothed update norm. The adaptive noise multiplier is
computed as
\[
\sigma_{k,t} =
\operatorname{clip}\left(
\sigma_0
\left(
1 + \beta \log\left(1 + \frac{s_{k,t}}{C_t}\right)
\right),
\sigma_{\min},
\sigma_{\max}
\right),
\]
where \(\sigma_0\) is the base noise multiplier, \(\beta\) controls the strength
of adaptation, and \(\sigma_{\min}\) and \(\sigma_{\max}\) bound the noise scale.

In adaptive clipping, the noise multiplier remains fixed while the clipping
threshold is updated over training. This allows the mechanism to adjust the
clipping behavior without changing the Gaussian noise multiplier.

\subsection{Membership Inference Evaluation}

To evaluate privacy leakage, we implement a white-box membership inference
attack. The attacker observes model-related information and attempts to infer
whether a given record was included in the training data. We train and evaluate
the attack model on held-out attack splits so that attack performance reflects
generalization rather than memorization.

We evaluate membership inference using ROC-AUC, where values near 0.5 indicate
chance-level attack performance. We also compare strict no-identifier
preprocessing against identifier-inclusive settings to measure the effect of
potentially leaky features.

\subsection{Utility Evaluation}

We evaluate intrusion-detection utility using accuracy, precision, recall,
F1-score, and ROC-AUC. Because both ECU-IoHT and WUSTL-EHMS 2020 are imbalanced,
we emphasize precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC rather than relying only
on accuracy. These metrics better reflect IDS behavior when malicious traffic is
rare or unevenly distributed across clients.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Datasets}

We evaluate the proposed federated intrusion-detection setting on two
IoHT-related datasets: ECU-IoHT and WUSTL-EHMS 2020. ECU-IoHT represents a
comparatively easier benchmark in our experiments, while WUSTL-EHMS 2020
provides a more difficult and imbalanced setting. For both datasets, we first
apply preprocessing to remove identifier-like fields where appropriate, since
such features can create artificial privacy leakage and inflate membership
inference performance.

After preprocessing, each dataset is divided into training, validation, test,
and attack-evaluation splits. The training split is used to construct federated
clients, while held-out data are used for utility evaluation and membership
inference analysis.

\subsection{Federated Client Partitioning}

Because ECU-IoHT and WUSTL-EHMS 2020 are centralized datasets, we simulate a
federated IoHT deployment by partitioning the training data into heterogeneous
clients. We use label-based Dirichlet partitioning, a common approach for
constructing non-IID federated learning benchmarks. For each class label,
samples are distributed across clients according to proportions drawn from
\[
p \sim \mathrm{Dirichlet}(\alpha).
\]

The concentration parameter \(\alpha\) controls the degree of client
heterogeneity. Larger values produce more uniform client label distributions,
while smaller values create stronger non-IID behavior. Following prior
federated learning experimental practice, we use \(\alpha=0.3\) to create a
deliberately heterogeneous setting. This value is commonly used as a stronger
non-IID condition compared with milder settings such as \(\alpha=0.6\).

In our experiments, we use \(K=8\) clients and enforce a minimum client size to
avoid degenerate clients with too few samples for local training. This produces
clients with different data volumes and different proportions of normal and
malicious traffic, which reflects the heterogeneous nature of IoHT environments.
Fig.~\ref{fig:client_split} shows the resulting client class composition for
ECU-IoHT and WUSTL-EHMS 2020.

\subsection{Federated Training Setup}

All privacy mechanisms are evaluated within the same federated learning
pipeline. At each communication round, the server sends the current global IDS
model to the clients. Each client trains locally on its private data and
computes a model update. The update is then either sent directly to the server
in the no-DP setting or privatized before upload in the DP-based settings. The
server aggregates client updates using FedAvg and broadcasts the updated global
model back to the clients.

To ensure a fair comparison, the model architecture, client split, number of
clients, and training configuration are kept fixed across privacy mechanisms for
each dataset. The only component varied is the client-side privacy mechanism.

\subsection{Privacy Mechanisms Compared}

We compare four training settings:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No DP:} client updates are aggregated without clipping or noise.
\item \textbf{Fixed-noise DP:} each client update is clipped and perturbed using a fixed Gaussian noise multiplier.
\item \textbf{Smoothed adaptive noise scaling:} the Gaussian noise multiplier is adjusted using a smoothed estimate of each client's update norm.
\item \textbf{Adaptive clipping:} the clipping threshold is adjusted during training while the noise multiplier remains fixed.
\end{itemize}

These settings allow us to compare a standard fixed-noise privacy baseline
against two adaptive variants: one that adapts the noise scale and one that
adapts the clipping threshold.

\subsection{Utility Metrics}

We evaluate intrusion-detection utility using accuracy, precision, recall,
F1-score, and ROC-AUC. Because both datasets are imbalanced, we emphasize
precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC rather than relying only on accuracy.
This is important for IoHT intrusion detection, where a high accuracy score can
hide poor detection of minority attack traffic.

\subsection{Membership Inference Evaluation}

To evaluate privacy leakage, we implement a white-box membership inference
attack. The attacker is trained to distinguish records used during model
training from held-out non-member records. Attack performance is evaluated on a
separate held-out attack split so that the reported results reflect attack
generalization rather than memorization.

We report membership inference performance using ROC-AUC. Values near 0.5
indicate chance-level attack behavior, while higher values indicate stronger
membership leakage. We also compare strict no-identifier preprocessing with
identifier-inclusive settings to examine how leaky features affect measured
privacy risk.

\section{Evaluation}

The evaluation compares utility and privacy behavior across the four training
settings: no-DP training, fixed-noise DP, smoothed adaptive noise scaling, and
adaptive clipping. Utility is measured using accuracy, precision, recall,
F1-score, and ROC-AUC. Privacy leakage is measured using white-box membership
inference ROC-AUC.

Across the current experimental settings, ECU-IoHT remains near ceiling across
privacy mechanisms. This limits the amount of measurable improvement adaptive
mechanisms can provide, because all methods already achieve strong utility. In
contrast, WUSTL-EHMS 2020 is more sensitive to privacy perturbations, making the
privacy--utility tradeoff easier to observe.

The membership inference results indicate that preprocessing choices strongly
affect measured privacy leakage. Under strict no-identifier preprocessing,
membership inference performance is close to chance. Identifier-inclusive WUSTL
serves as a leakage-positive control and demonstrates that identifier-like
features can substantially inflate measured privacy risk.

Overall, the adaptive privacy mechanisms do not provide consistent or
substantial utility gains over fixed-noise DP in the current experimental
configuration. These results suggest that adaptive mechanisms may require more
challenging benchmarks, better-calibrated privacy parameters, or more detailed
client heterogeneity modeling to produce meaningful and reliable improvements.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper studied fixed and adaptive client-side privacy mechanisms for
federated IoHT intrusion detection under heterogeneous non-IID clients. We
compared no-DP training, fixed-noise DP, smoothed adaptive noise scaling, and
adaptive clipping on ECU-IoHT and WUSTL-EHMS 2020. We also evaluated privacy
leakage using a white-box membership inference attack.

Our results suggest that adaptive privacy mechanisms do not consistently improve
utility over fixed-noise DP in the current settings. ECU-IoHT remains near
ceiling across mechanisms, while WUSTL-EHMS 2020 is more sensitive to privacy
noise. Membership inference results are strongly affected by preprocessing:
strict no-identifier preprocessing reduces attack performance close to chance,
whereas identifier-inclusive settings expose stronger leakage.

Future work should evaluate adaptive privacy mechanisms on more challenging
IoHT intrusion-detection benchmarks, perform broader privacy-parameter sweeps,
and study client-specific adaptation strategies that better account for
heterogeneous data size, class balance, and update behavior.
