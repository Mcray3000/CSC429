\begin{abstract}
Federated learning (FL) is a promising approach for intrusion detection in
Internet-of-Healthcare-Things (IoHT) networks because it allows clients to train
collaboratively without centralizing sensitive data. However, client model
updates can still leak information about local training records. Differential
privacy (DP) can reduce this risk, but fixed privacy settings may be poorly
suited for heterogeneous IoHT clients with non-IID data.

In this work, we evaluate fixed and adaptive client-side privacy mechanisms for
federated IoHT intrusion detection. We simulate heterogeneous clients using
Dirichlet-based partitioning on the ECU-IoHT and WUSTL-EHMS 2020 datasets, and
compare no-DP training, fixed-noise DP, smoothed adaptive noise scaling, and
adaptive clipping. We evaluate detection utility using precision, recall,
F1-score, ROC-AUC, and PR-AUC, and assess privacy leakage using a white-box
membership inference attack.

Our results show that strict no-identifier preprocessing reduces membership
inference performance close to chance across privacy mechanisms.
Utility-preserving DP settings require very small noise multipliers,
corresponding to weak approximate per-update privacy scales, while stronger
target epsilon settings require much larger noise and cause utility collapse.
Overall, adaptive mechanisms provide utility comparable to fixed-noise DP in our
evaluated settings, suggesting that adaptive privacy has limited room to improve
when empirical leakage is already low and stronger noise is not
utility-tolerable.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Federated learning, differential privacy, IoHT, intrusion detection, membership

\section{Introduction}

Internet-of-Healthcare-Things (IoHT) devices are increasingly used to monitor
patients, transmit medical data, and support connected healthcare services. The
data generated by these systems can improve machine learning-based intrusion
detection, but centralizing sensitive medical and network data raises privacy
concerns. Federated learning (FL) reduces this risk by allowing clients to train
locally and share model updates rather than raw
data~\cite{mcmahan2017communication,prayitno2021healthcarefl}. However, model
updates can still leak information about local training samples.

Differential privacy (DP) is commonly used to reduce such leakage by perturbing
client updates before aggregation. Many DP-based FL methods apply a fixed noise
level to all clients, but this is limiting for heterogeneous IoHT clients with
different data volumes, class balances, and traffic distributions. In intrusion
detection, where malicious samples may be rare and unevenly distributed, a
single fixed privacy setting can produce an unfavorable privacy--utility
tradeoff.

Adaptive privacy mechanisms address this limitation by adjusting perturbation
across clients or training rounds. Prior work has shown the promise of adaptive
local DP in FL, but often on general image-classification or IoT benchmarks
rather than heterogeneous IoHT intrusion-detection
datasets~\cite{yue2025adapldpfl,he2024clustered,talaei2024adaptive}. Thus, the
practical benefit of adaptive privacy for federated IoHT intrusion detection
remains under-characterized.

In this work, we evaluate fixed and adaptive client-side privacy mechanisms for
federated IoHT intrusion detection. We use Dirichlet-based partitioning to
simulate non-IID clients on ECU-IoHT and WUSTL-EHMS 2020, compare fixed and
adaptive privacy mechanisms, and evaluate both IDS utility and white-box
membership leakage.

Our main contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We evaluate fixed and adaptive client-side privacy mechanisms under
    heterogeneous, non-IID federated IoHT clients.
    \item We compare privacy--utility behavior on ECU-IoHT and WUSTL-EHMS 2020
    using IDS metrics and white-box membership inference.
    \item We find that adaptive privacy mechanisms provide utility comparable to
    fixed-noise DP in our current settings, while membership inference results
    are strongly influenced by preprocessing choices such as identifier removal.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section II reviews related
work. Section III presents the methodology. Section IV describes the experiments
and results. Section V concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}

Federated learning has been widely studied for distributed learning when raw
data cannot be centralized~\cite{mcmahan2017communication}. In healthcare and
IoHT environments, FL is attractive because medical and network traffic data may
contain sensitive information, and recent surveys identify non-IID data and data
protection as major challenges for healthcare
FL~\cite{prayitno2021healthcarefl}. Recent IoHT intrusion-detection work has
also combined FL with DP to protect healthcare network data while training IDS
models~\cite{mosaiyebzadeh2025secioht}.

Differential privacy is commonly used to mitigate update leakage by clipping
model updates and adding calibrated noise before aggregation. Gaussian noise is
a standard mechanism for achieving approximate \((\epsilon,\delta)\)-DP, with
the noise scale determined by the sensitivity of the released quantity and the
target privacy parameters~\cite{dwork2006ourdata,dwork2014algorithmic}. Local DP
has also been studied in federated IoT settings, where privacy budgets and noise
levels directly affect model utility~\cite{zhao2021ldpfl}. Adaptive DP methods
attempt to improve this tradeoff by changing clipping thresholds or noise levels
across clients or training
rounds~\cite{yue2025adapldpfl,he2024clustered,talaei2024adaptive}.

Membership inference attacks provide a practical way to evaluate empirical
privacy leakage. In these attacks, an adversary attempts to determine whether a
specific record was included in the model's training
data~\cite{shokri2017membership}. White-box attacks are especially relevant in
federated settings because the adversary may observe model parameters,
gradients, losses, or other training-related signals.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{System Model}

We consider a federated IoHT intrusion-detection setting with \(K\)
heterogeneous clients. Each client represents a local healthcare environment,
such as a glucose-monitoring system, blood-pressure monitoring system, or
patient-monitoring system. Client \(k\) holds a private local dataset \(D_k\),
which may differ from other clients in size, class balance, and traffic
distribution. Raw IoHT traffic and training records remain local throughout
training.

A central federated server coordinates training but does not receive raw data.
In each communication round, clients train local IDS models, compute model
updates, apply the assigned privacy mechanism, and send only the resulting
updates to the server. The server aggregates these updates to produce a global
IDS model, which is then redistributed to the clients.

\subsection{Threat Model}

We consider two related threats. First, IoHT clients may observe malicious
network behavior, motivating intrusion detection. Second, a privacy adversary
may attempt to infer whether a specific record was used during local model
training. We focus on this second threat using a white-box membership inference
attack.

The adversary may observe model-related information used by the attack model,
such as model outputs, losses, gradients, or update-derived signals, but does
not access raw client data. The adversary's goal is to distinguish member
records from non-member records. We do not study poisoning, Byzantine clients,
denial-of-service attacks, or communication-layer compromise.

\subsection{Federated Learning Formulation}

Let \(\theta^t\) denote the global model parameters at round \(t\). The server
broadcasts \(\theta^t\) to the participating clients. Each client trains locally
and obtains updated parameters \(\theta_k^t\). The client update is
\[
\Delta_k^t = \theta_k^t - \theta^t.
\]

The server aggregates client updates using FedAvg:
\[
\theta^{t+1} = \theta^t + \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k \widetilde{\Delta}_k^t,
\]
where \(w_k\) is the aggregation weight for client \(k\), and
\(\widetilde{\Delta}_k^t\) is the update received by the server. In the no-DP
setting, \(\widetilde{\Delta}_k^t=\Delta_k^t\). In DP-based settings, the update
is clipped and noised before upload.

\subsection{Client-Side Privacy Mechanisms}

For DP-based methods, each client update is first clipped:
\[
\bar{\Delta}_k^t =
\Delta_k^t \cdot
\min\left(1, \frac{C_t}{\left\lVert \Delta_k^t \right\rVert_2}\right),
\]
where \(C_t\) is the clipping threshold. Gaussian noise is then added:
\[
\widetilde{\Delta}_k^t =
\bar{\Delta}_k^t + \mathcal{N}\left(0, \sigma_{k,t}^{2} C_t^{2} I\right).
\]

This privacy mechanism follows the standard Gaussian mechanism. For a function
with \(\ell_2\)-sensitivity \(\Delta_2(f)\), the Gaussian mechanism provides
\((\epsilon,\delta)\)-DP when the added Gaussian noise has standard deviation
proportional to \(\Delta_2(f)/\epsilon\), with the usual bound requiring \(c^2 >
2\ln(1.25/\delta)\) and
\[
\sigma \geq \frac{c\Delta_2(f)}{\epsilon}
\]
for \(\epsilon \in (0,1)\)~\cite{dwork2006ourdata,dwork2014algorithmic}. In our
implementation, clipping bounds the update scale through \(C_t\), while the
Gaussian noise is controlled by the multiplier \(\sigma_{k,t}\). Thus, larger
\(\sigma_{k,t}\) corresponds to a smaller approximate \(\epsilon\), but also
stronger perturbation of the client update. We use this relationship as an
approximate per-update privacy scale, not as a composed privacy accountant
across FL rounds.

We compare three DP variants. In fixed-noise DP, all clients use the same noise
multiplier, \(\sigma_{k,t}=\sigma\). In smoothed adaptive noise scaling, the
noise multiplier changes using a smoothed update-norm estimate:
\[
s_{k,t} = \lambda s_{k,t-1} + (1-\lambda)\left\lVert \Delta_k^t \right\rVert_2,
\]
\[
\sigma_{k,t} =
\operatorname{clip}\left(
\sigma_0
\left(
1 + \beta \log\left(1 + \frac{s_{k,t}}{C_t}\right)
\right),
\sigma_{\min},
\sigma_{\max}
\right).
\]
In adaptive clipping, the noise multiplier remains fixed while the clipping
threshold is adjusted during training.

\section{Experimental Setup and Results}

\subsection{Datasets and Preprocessing}

We evaluate on ECU-IoHT and WUSTL-EHMS 2020. ECU-IoHT was introduced as a
dataset for analyzing cyberattacks in Internet of Health Things environments,
while WUSTL-EHMS 2020 was created from an enhanced healthcare monitoring system
testbed that includes network-flow and biometric
measurements~\cite{ahmed2021ecuiot,wustl2020ehms}. These datasets have also been
used in recent privacy-preserving FL-based IoHT IDS
work~\cite{mosaiyebzadeh2025secioht}. In our experiments, ECU-IoHT serves as a
comparatively separable benchmark, while WUSTL-EHMS 2020 provides a more
sensitive test of privacy perturbation and class imbalance.

For the main evaluation, we apply strict no-identifier preprocessing by removing
identifier-like fields such as IDs, MAC-like features, and other direct or
quasi-identifiers. This is important because identifier-like features can create
leakage unrelated to the federated privacy mechanism itself. We also evaluate an
identifier-inclusive WUSTL setting as a leakage-positive control to verify that
the membership inference attack detects leakage when leaky features are present.

\subsection{Federated Client Partitioning}

Because both datasets are centralized, we simulate federated clients using
label-based Dirichlet partitioning. For each class, samples are distributed
across \(K\) clients according to
\[
p \sim \mathrm{Dirichlet}(\alpha).
\]
The concentration parameter \(\alpha\) controls the degree of non-IID behavior:
smaller values create more uneven client label distributions. This partitioning
choice follows common federated learning practice for constructing non-IID
client populations. For example, the FedDC supplemental evaluates Dirichlet
settings with \(\alpha=0.3\) and \(\alpha=0.6\), and identifies
Dirichlet-\(0.3\) as the stronger non-IID condition~\cite{gao2022feddc_supp}.

In our experiments, we use \(K=8\) clients and \(\alpha=0.3\) to deliberately
create heterogeneous client sizes and class balances. This choice is appropriate
for our IoHT setting because healthcare clients may observe different traffic
volumes and different proportions of normal and malicious activity.
Fig.~\ref{fig:client_split} shows that the resulting split produces uneven
client class compositions for both ECU-IoHT and WUSTL-EHMS 2020.

\subsection{Training Conditions and Metrics}

We compare four training settings: no-DP training, fixed-noise DP, smoothed
adaptive noise scaling, and adaptive clipping. All settings use the same client
splits, model architecture, and FL pipeline so that differences are attributable
to the privacy mechanism.

We evaluate IDS utility using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, ROC-AUC,
and PR-AUC. Because IoHT intrusion-detection data are imbalanced, we emphasize
F1-score, ROC-AUC, and PR-AUC rather than accuracy alone. We evaluate privacy
leakage using white-box membership inference ROC-AUC, where values near 0.5
indicate chance-level attack performance.

\subsection{Fixed-Noise Utility Sweep}

To understand how each dataset responds to increasing privacy noise, we perform
a fixed-noise sweep under strict no-identifier preprocessing. This sweep varies
the Gaussian noise multiplier and measures F1-score and PR-AUC.

Fig.~\ref{fig:ecu_noise_sweep} shows that ECU-IoHT remains near the utility
ceiling across the tested noise range. Both F1-score and PR-AUC remain high,
indicating that the dataset is relatively separable under the current
preprocessing and model configuration. This limits the room for any privacy
mechanism, fixed or adaptive, to improve measured utility.

\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:wustl_noise_sweep} shows a different pattern for WUSTL-EHMS 2020.

\subsection{Membership Inference Results}

Fig.~\ref{fig:mia_auc} shows the white-box membership inference ROC-AUC under
strict no-identifier preprocessing. For both ECU-IoHT and WUSTL-EHMS 2020,
attack performance remains close to the chance line across no-DP, fixed-noise
DP, smoothed adaptive noise scaling, and adaptive clipping. This indicates that,
after identifier removal, there is little measurable membership leakage under
the evaluated attack.

This result is central to interpreting the DP experiments. Since no-DP training
is already near chance under strict preprocessing, DP has limited room to
further reduce empirical membership inference risk. Therefore, changes in the
privacy mechanism are mainly reflected in utility behavior rather than a large
change in measured MIA AUC.

\end{figure}

\subsection{Approximate Privacy Scale}

Table~\ref{tab:epsilon_sigma_summary} summarizes the approximate
Gaussian-mechanism privacy scale used to interpret the tested noise multipliers.
The relationship above shows why the noise multiplier and privacy scale move in
opposite directions: smaller \(\sigma\) preserves the client update but implies
a larger approximate \(\epsilon\), while larger \(\sigma\) gives a stronger
privacy scale but perturbs the update more heavily. The utility-preserving
settings in our experiments use very small noise multipliers around \(\sigma
\approx 0.010\), corresponding to very large approximate per-update \(\epsilon\)
values. These settings preserve IDS utility but should be interpreted as weak
formal privacy scales under this Gaussian-mechanism estimate.

We also tested whether more conventional target \(\epsilon\) values could be
used by increasing the noise multiplier. Targeting \(\epsilon=2\)--8 required
much larger noise multipliers, approximately \(0.43\)--\(1.65\), and these
settings caused utility to collapse. At the same time, membership inference
remained near chance because strict no-identifier preprocessing had already
removed most measurable leakage. Thus, simply increasing privacy noise did not
reveal a useful adaptive-DP regime: the stronger settings damaged utility, while
the empirical MIA signal remained low.

\subsection{Fixed and Adaptive Privacy Comparison}

Across the evaluated settings, adaptive privacy mechanisms provide utility
comparable to fixed-noise DP. ECU-IoHT remains near ceiling across mechanisms,
while WUSTL-EHMS 2020 is more sensitive to DP perturbations. The fixed-noise
sweeps in Fig.~\ref{fig:ecu_noise_sweep} and Fig.~\ref{fig:wustl_noise_sweep}
show that increasing noise is not equally tolerable across datasets.

The MIA results in Fig.~\ref{fig:mia_auc} explain why adaptive mechanisms do not
show a large empirical privacy advantage in the current setting. After strict
preprocessing, measured membership leakage is already near chance, so changing
the privacy mechanism does not substantially change attack AUC. The epsilon
sensitivity in Table~\ref{tab:epsilon_sigma_summary} shows that target
\(\epsilon=2\)--8 requires much larger noise multipliers than the
utility-preserving runs. This matches the fixed-noise sweep in
Fig.~\ref{fig:wustl_noise_sweep}: when the noise multiplier moves into that
larger range, WUSTL-EHMS F1-score and PR-AUC degrade substantially. Together,
these results identify a specific limitation: adaptive DP had little room to
improve privacy on these cleaned datasets because empirical leakage was already
low, while stronger perturbation destroyed utility.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper evaluated fixed and adaptive client-side privacy mechanisms for
federated IoHT intrusion detection under heterogeneous non-IID clients. We
compared no-DP training, fixed-noise DP, smoothed adaptive noise scaling, and
adaptive clipping on ECU-IoHT and WUSTL-EHMS 2020, and evaluated privacy leakage
using a white-box membership inference attack.

Our results show that strict no-identifier preprocessing reduces membership
inference performance close to chance across all evaluated privacy mechanisms.
In terms of utility, ECU-IoHT remains near ceiling, while WUSTL-EHMS 2020 is
more sensitive to privacy noise. Utility-preserving DP settings require very
small noise multipliers, while target \(\epsilon=2\)--8 settings require much
larger noise and collapse utility. Therefore, adaptive mechanisms provide
utility comparable to fixed-noise DP in this study not simply because the
datasets are insufficiently challenging, but because the evaluated regime leaves
little empirical membership leakage to reduce and little utility tolerance for
stronger perturbation.

Future work should focus on settings where adaptive behavior can be evaluated
more directly, such as drift-based adaptive clipping, client-specific update
diagnostics, and benchmarks or splits with measurable leakage after
preprocessing but enough utility tolerance for meaningful DP noise.

\section{Ethical Considerations}

This work aims to improve privacy-preserving intrusion detection for IoHT
systems. We reduce exposure of sensitive data by keeping raw records local,
removing identifier-like fields, and reporting results in aggregate. The
membership inference attack is used only to evaluate privacy leakage, not to
identify individuals or attack operational healthcare systems.
