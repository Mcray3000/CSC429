\begin{abstract}
Federated learning can reduce raw data sharing in Internet-of-Healthcare-Things
(IoHT) intrusion detection, but client model updates may still leak training
information. This work evaluates fixed and adaptive client-side differential
privacy (DP) for federated IoHT intrusion detection using ECU-IoHT and
WUSTL-EHMS 2020 with Dirichlet-based non-IID client splits. We compare no-DP,
fixed-noise DP, smoothed adaptive noise scaling, and adaptive clipping using IDS
utility metrics and a white-box membership inference attack. Our main finding is
that adaptive DP did not provide a clear advantage in this experimental setting.
Strict no-identifier preprocessing reduced membership inference AUC close to
chance, leaving little measurable leakage for DP to further reduce, while
epsilon-scale noise large enough to provide stronger approximate privacy caused
utility collapse. ECU-IoHT also remained near the utility ceiling, while
WUSTL-EHMS exposed stronger noise sensitivity. Our results show that not every
IoHT dataset or preprocessing approach is informative for demonstrating adaptive
privacy gains. We find that adaptive mechanisms need both measurable leakage and
utility tolerance for stronger perturbation to show benefit.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Federated learning, differential privacy, IoHT, intrusion detection, membership
inference, non-IID data, privacy--utility tradeoff
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Internet-of-Healthcare-Things (IoHT) devices generate sensitive medical and
network traffic data that can support machine learning-based intrusion
detection. Centralizing this data raises privacy concerns, so federated learning
(FL) is attractive because clients train locally and share model updates rather
than raw records~\cite{mcmahan2017communication,prayitno2021healthcarefl}.
However, model updates can still reveal information about local training data.

Differential privacy (DP) reduces update leakage by clipping updates and adding
noise before aggregation. Many DP-FL methods use one fixed noise setting for all
clients. IoHT clients may differ in data size, class balance, and traffic
distribution. In healthcare settings, stronger privacy protection is especially
important to protect sensitive patient data. However, intrusion detection also
has to remain accurate enough to identify attacks. This creates a difficult
privacy–utility tradeoff. Specifically, stronger DP noise can improve privacy
protection, but excessive noise may reduce detection performance, while weaker
noise may preserve IDS utility but provide weaker formal privacy. Adaptive
privacy mechanisms attempt to improve this tradeoff by adjusting noise or
clipping across clients or rounds. Prior work has studied adaptive local DP in
FL and IoT settings~\cite{yue2025adapldpfl,he2024clustered,talaei2024adaptive}.
However, for heterogeneous IoHT intrusion-detection data, its practical benefit
is still under-characterized.

In this work, we evaluate fixed and adaptive client-side privacy mechanisms on
ECU-IoHT and WUSTL-EHMS 2020. We then simulate heterogeneous clients using
Dirichlet-based partitioning, measure IDS utility using F1-score, and PR-AUC.
Finally, we evaluate and report empirical privacy leakage using a white-box
membership inference attack all with our proposed methodology.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We evaluate fixed and adaptive client-side privacy mechanisms under
    heterogeneous, non-IID federated IoHT clients.
    \item We compare ECU-IoHT and WUSTL-EHMS 2020 using both IDS utility metrics
    and white-box membership inference.
    \item We show that adaptive DP provides utility comparable to fixed-noise DP
    in our setting because strict preprocessing leaves little measurable
    membership leakage, while stronger noise calibrated for stronger privacy
    collapses utility.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section II reviews related
work. Section III presents the methodology. Section IV describes the experiments
and results. Section V concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}

FL has been widely used for distributed learning when raw data cannot be
centralized~\cite{mcmahan2017communication}. In healthcare, FL is motivated by
sensitive data and non-IID client populations~\cite{prayitno2021healthcarefl}.
Recent IoHT intrusion-detection work has also combined FL with privacy
mechanisms to protect healthcare network data during IDS
training~\cite{mosaiyebzadeh2025secioht}.

DP provides formal privacy protection by adding calibrated noise to a function
output. The Gaussian mechanism gives approximate \((\epsilon,\delta)\)-DP, with
noise determined by sensitivity, \(\epsilon\), and
\(\delta\)~\cite{dwork2006ourdata,dwork2014algorithmic}. Local DP has been
studied in federated IoT settings, where privacy budget and noise level directly
affect utility~\cite{zhao2021ldpfl}. Adaptive DP methods try to improve this
balance by changing noise or clipping based on client or training
behavior~\cite{yue2025adapldpfl,he2024clustered,talaei2024adaptive}.

Membership inference attacks evaluate whether a trained model reveals that a
specific record was included in training~\cite{shokri2017membership}. This
threat is important in federated learning. FL does reduce raw data sharing, but
it does not eliminate leakage through model updates, gradients, or final model
behavior. In IoHT intrusion detection, membership leakage is especially
concerning because records may correspond to sensitive healthcare-device traffic
that can involve patient data.

White-box membership inference provides a stronger evaluation setting than
black-box attacks because the adversary can use internal model signals rather
than only predicted labels or confidence scores. These signals may include
losses, gradients, logits, or update-derived features, all of which can expose
differences between records seen during training and records held out from
training. For this reason, white-box MIA is useful for evaluating whether
privacy mechanisms reduce observable training-record leakage in the actual FL
pipeline.

However, MIA results depend strongly on the dataset and preprocessing choices.
Identifier-like features, duplicated patterns, or highly client-specific traffic
can increase attack success because the model may learn shortcuts that separate
training records from non-training records. Conversely, removing identifiers and
other directly identifying fields can reduce attack signal before DP is even
applied. This distinction is important because a low MIA AUC after preprocessing
does not necessarily mean that DP caused the reduction. It instead may indicate
that the preprocessing pipeline already removed much of the measurable
membership signal.

Therefore, empirical MIA evaluation and formal DP analysis answer different
questions. DP characterizes the theoretical protection provided by a randomized
mechanism under assumptions about sensitivity, noise, composition, and privacy
accounting. MIA measures whether a particular attacker can exploit the trained
model or update signals under a specific dataset, preprocessing strategy, and
training procedure. Evaluating both is necessary because a method may preserve
utility and reduce empirical leakage while still providing weak formal privacy,
or it may provide stronger formal privacy only at a utility cost that makes the
IDS unusable.

This gap motivates our evaluation. Rather than assuming that adaptive DP will
improve the privacy--utility tradeoff, we test whether adaptive noise scaling
and adaptive clipping provide measurable benefit over fixed-noise DP under
heterogeneous IoHT client splits. This allows us to separate three effects.
First, whether the datasets contain measurable membership leakage. Secondly, DP
noise reduces that leakage. And lastly, whether adaptive mechanisms preserve IDS
utility better than fixed privacy settings.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{System Model}

We consider a federated IoHT intrusion-detection setting with \(K\)
heterogeneous clients. Each client represents a local healthcare environment,
such as a glucose-monitoring, blood-pressure monitoring, or patient-monitoring
system, as indicated by Fig.~\ref{fig:system_model}.

Client \(k\) holds a private local dataset \(D_k\), which may differ from other
clients in size, class balance, and traffic distribution. Raw traffic remains
local. In each communication round, clients train local IDS models, compute
updates, apply the assigned privacy mechanism, and send only the resulting
updates to the server. The server aggregates the updates using FedAvg and
redistributes the updated global IDS model.

\subsection{Threat Model}

We consider the privacy threat that an adversary attempts to infer whether a
record was used during local training. The adversary may observe model-related
signals used by the white-box attack, such as model outputs, losses, gradients,
or update-derived features, but does not directly access raw client records.
This reflects a federated setting where raw IoHT traffic remains local, but
shared model behavior may still reveal information about individual samples. The
attacker's goal is to distinguish member records from non-member records, and we
measure success using membership inference ROC-AUC.


\subsection{Federated Learning Formulation}

Let \(\theta^t\) denote the global model parameters at round \(t\). The server
broadcasts \(\theta^t\), and each client trains locally to obtain
\(\theta_k^t\). The client update is
\[
\Delta_k^t = \theta_k^t - \theta^t.
\]
The server aggregates updates using FedAvg:
\[
\theta^{t+1} = \theta^t + \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k \widetilde{\Delta}_k^t,
\]
where \(w_k\) is the aggregation weight and \(\widetilde{\Delta}_k^t\) is the
update received by the server. In the no-DP setting,
\(\widetilde{\Delta}_k^t=\Delta_k^t\). In DP settings, the update is clipped and
noised before upload.

\subsection{Client-Side Privacy Mechanisms}

For DP-based methods, each client update is clipped:
\[
\bar{\Delta}_k^t =
\Delta_k^t \cdot
\min\left(1, \frac{C_t}{\left\lVert \Delta_k^t \right\rVert_2}\right),
\]
where \(C_t\) is the clipping threshold. Gaussian noise is then added:
\[
\widetilde{\Delta}_k^t =
\bar{\Delta}_k^t + \mathcal{N}\left(0, \sigma_{k,t}^{2} C_t^{2} I\right).
\]

The Gaussian mechanism links noise, sensitivity, and privacy. For a function
with \(\ell_2\)-sensitivity \(\Delta_2(f)\), adding Gaussian noise with standard
deviation
\[
\sigma \geq \frac{c\Delta_2(f)}{\epsilon},
\quad c^2 > 2\ln(1.25/\delta),
\]
gives \((\epsilon,\delta)\)-DP for \(\epsilon \in
(0,1)\)~\cite{dwork2006ourdata,dwork2014algorithmic}. In our update mechanism,
clipping sets the sensitivity scale through \(C_t\), and the actual noise
standard deviation is proportional to \(\sigma_{k,t}C_t\). Thus, for fixed
\(C_t\) and \(\delta\), increasing \(\sigma_{k,t}\) decreases the approximate
per-update \(\epsilon\), but also perturbs the IDS update more strongly. A full
FL privacy budget would additionally depend on client sampling, number of
rounds, composition, and the accountant used; here we report only approximate
per-update privacy scales.

We compare three DP variants. In fixed-noise DP, all clients use the same
\(\sigma_{k,t}=\sigma\). In smoothed adaptive noise scaling,
\[
s_{k,t} = \lambda s_{k,t-1} + (1-\lambda)\left\lVert \Delta_k^t \right\rVert_2,
\]
\[
\sigma_{k,t} =
\operatorname{clip}\left(
\sigma_0
\left(
1 + \beta \log\left(1 + \frac{s_{k,t}}{C_t}\right)
\right),
\sigma_{\min},
\sigma_{\max}
\right).
\]
In adaptive clipping, \(\sigma\) remains fixed while \(C_t\) changes during
training.

\section{Experimental Setup and Results}

\subsection{Datasets and Preprocessing}

We evaluate on ECU-IoHT and WUSTL-EHMS 2020. ECU-IoHT was introduced for
analyzing cyberattacks in Internet of Health Things environments. ECU-IoHT is
built from a controlled, simulated testbed. It contains eight to nine core
features with over 111,207 total records. The simulated devices are connected
through Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and local subnets. Meanwhile, WUSTL-EHMS 2020 was
created from real network traffic, including real physiological data captured
from an Enhanced Healthcare Monitoring System
testbed~\cite{ahmed2021ecuiot,wustl2020ehms}. It contains 44 features, including
eight biometric and physiological features such as pulse rate, blood pressure,
and oxygen saturation. Both have been used in privacy-preserving FL-based IoHT
IDS work~\cite{mosaiyebzadeh2025secioht}.

For the main evaluation, we remove identifier-like fields including IDs,
MAC-like features, and direct identifiers. We also use an identifier-inclusive
WUSTL condition as a positive-control leakage condition to confirm that the
membership inference attack detects leakage when leaky features are retained.

\subsection{Federated Client Partitioning}

The datasets are centralized, so we simulate federated clients using label-based
Dirichlet partitioning:
\[
p \sim \mathrm{Dirichlet}(\alpha).
\]
Smaller \(\alpha\) values create more uneven client label distributions. This
matches common non-IID FL evaluation practice; the FedDC supplemental uses
\(\alpha=0.3\) and \(\alpha=0.6\), with \(0.3\) representing the stronger
non-IID setting~\cite{gao2022feddc_supp}. We use \(K=8\) clients and
\(\alpha=0.3\) to create heterogeneous client sizes and class balances.
Fig.~\ref{fig:client_split} shows the resulting split.

\subsection{Training Conditions and Metrics}

We compare no-DP training, fixed-noise DP, smoothed adaptive noise scaling, and
adaptive clipping. All conditions use the same client splits and FL pipeline so
that differences are attributable to the privacy mechanism.

We evaluate IDS utility using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and PR-AUC.
Because the datasets are imbalanced, we emphasize F1-score, ROC-AUC, and PR-AUC.
We evaluate privacy leakage using white-box membership inference ROC-AUC, where
0.5 indicates chance-level attack performance.

\subsection{Privacy Evaluation and Budget Sensitivity}

Membership inference is used as an empirical leakage test. Member samples come
from records used to train the target model, and non-member samples come from
held-out records. We report attack ROC-AUC, where 0.5 indicates chance-level
performance. We evaluate the attack mainly after identifier removal so that the
measured leakage reflects model behavior rather than direct identifier leakage;
the identifier-inclusive WUSTL condition is used only as a positive control to
confirm that the attack detects leakage when leaky features are present.

The privacy scale also depends on more than the noise multiplier alone. Under
the Gaussian-mechanism estimate, clipping controls the sensitivity scale through
\(C\), while the added noise is proportional to \(\sigma C\). For fixed
\(\delta\), larger \(\sigma\) lowers approximate per-update \(\epsilon\), but
also perturbs the client update more strongly. A full training-level privacy
budget would also require composition across FL rounds and client sampling,
which we do not compute here. Therefore, we interpret \(\epsilon\) only as an
approximate per-update scale used to compare noise settings.

\subsection{Fixed-Noise Utility Sweep}

We first vary the fixed Gaussian noise multiplier under strict no-identifier
preprocessing. This tests whether each dataset can tolerate the noise needed for
DP.

Fig.~\ref{fig:ecu_noise_sweep} shows that ECU-IoHT remains near the utility
ceiling across the tested noise range. Since F1-score and PR-AUC are already
high, ECU-IoHT provides little room for adaptive mechanisms to show utility
improvement.

\subsection{Approximate Privacy Scale}

Table~\ref{tab:epsilon_sigma_summary} summarizes the approximate relationship
between the tested noise multipliers and per-update Gaussian-mechanism privacy
scale. With \(C=1.0\) and \(\delta=10^{-5}\), the utility-preserving settings
use very small noise multipliers around \(\sigma \approx 0.010\). These values
preserve IDS performance but correspond to very large approximate \(\epsilon\),
meaning weak formal privacy scale.

Targeting more conventional \(\epsilon=2\)--8 required much larger noise
multipliers, approximately \(0.43\)--\(1.65\). These values match the high-noise
range where WUSTL utility degrades in Fig.~\ref{fig:wustl_noise_sweep}. Thus,
the experiment reveal the tradeoff that the noise needed for stronger
approximate privacy is not utility-tolerable in this setting, while lower noise
preserves utility but gives weak formal privacy scale.

\subsection{Interpretation}

Adaptive DP did not show a clear advantage because the evaluated datasets leave
little room for it to help. First, strict preprocessing removed most measurable
membership leakage, so MIA AUC stayed near chance even without DP. Second, the
noise multipliers that preserved utility produced weak approximate privacy
scales. Third, increasing noise to reach \(\epsilon=2\)--8 moved into the range
where WUSTL utility collapsed entirely.

This means the result is not simply that adaptive DP failed or that the datasets
were ``too easy.'' Rather, these datasets and preprocessing choices created a
regime where privacy leakage was already empirically low, but stronger
formal-noise settings were too damaging for IDS utility. Under these conditions,
adaptive mechanisms can match fixed DP but have limited opportunity to improve
the privacy--utility tradeoff.

\section{Conclusion}

This work evaluated fixed and adaptive client-side DP for federated IoHT
intrusion detection under heterogeneous non-IID clients. We found that adaptive
DP provided utility comparable to fixed-noise DP, but did not produce a clear
privacy or utility advantage in the evaluated regime.

The main limitation of this study is that strict no-identifier preprocessing
reduced MIA AUC close to chance, leaving little measurable membership leakage
for DP to reduce. In addition, the noise levels that preserved utility were too
small to provide strong approximate privacy, while targeting \(\epsilon=2\)--8
required much larger noise and caused sharp utility degradation.

Future work should focus on drift-based adaptive clipping, client-specific
update diagnostics, and settings where privacy improvement can be measured
directly. In particular, future experiments should use datasets or client splits
with observable post-cleaning membership leakage and enough utility tolerance
for meaningful DP noise, allowing adaptive privacy to be tested against
fixed-noise DP without immediate utility collapse.

\section{Ethical Considerations}

This work aims to improve privacy-preserving IoHT intrusion detection. We keep
raw records local, remove identifier-like fields, and report aggregate results.
The membership inference attack is used only to evaluate privacy leakage.
